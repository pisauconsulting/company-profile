5 Signs Your Restaurant Is Losing Money Without Knowing It

6 min read · Food cost control

Most F&B operators we meet in Bali do not have a revenue problem. The covers are there, the room looks busy, the reviews are good. And yet the month closes thinner than it should, and nobody can say exactly why.

That is almost never one large leak. It is usually five small ones running at the same time, none of them visible from the P&L until the money has already gone. By the time a number looks wrong on a monthly report, the decisions that caused it were made six weeks earlier, by people who had no way of seeing the consequence.

Here are the five we find most often, and the specific check that exposes each one.

01You know your food cost percentage, but not your food cost variance

A food cost percentage tells you what happened. It does not tell you why. Two venues can both report 34% and be in completely different health.

The number that matters is the gap between theoretical cost — what the dishes you actually sold should have cost, according to your recipes — and actual cost, what you actually spent. That gap is where the money goes: over-portioning, unrecorded waste, staff meals nobody logged, free rounds, theft.

Most venues cannot calculate this at all, because calculating theoretical cost requires costed recipes, and costed recipes are the thing everybody postpones. So they are left with a single monthly number and no way to interrogate it.

The check

Take your twenty highest-volume dishes. Cost them properly, down to the gram, including trim loss. Multiply by units sold last month. Compare that figure to what you actually spent on those ingredients. The difference is not an accounting error — it is an operational one, and it has a cause you can name.

02Supplier prices move and nobody notices

Supplier pricing drifts. Rarely in dramatic jumps — in quiet increments, on individual lines, spread across months. A core protein goes up, a dairy line goes up, a packaging item goes up. Each increase on its own is too small for anyone to escalate.

The symptom is unmistakable once you know to look for it: same menu, same volume, same portioning discipline, higher cost of goods. Nothing changed in the kitchen, so the kitchen is not where the answer is.

This is a purchasing visibility problem, not a negotiation problem. You cannot renegotiate a price increase you never registered.

The check

Rank your purchase lines by total monthly spend and take the top twenty. Put last month's unit price next to this month's. Anything that moved more than a few percent without a conversation is a line you are now paying more for by default.

03You count stock, but nothing changes afterwards

Counting inventory is not inventory control. A stock take only becomes useful at the moment you compare it against what your recipes predicted you should be holding. Without that comparison you have spent a Sunday morning producing a number nobody acts on.

The second half of this is dead stock, and it is the one owners underestimate most. Every item sitting in your store with no movement in sixty days is cash you already spent, parked on a shelf, quietly expiring. It does not appear as a loss anywhere until you write it off — and most venues never formally write anything off, so it simply never surfaces.

The check

Pull every SKU with zero movement in the last sixty days and add up what you paid for it. That total is working capital you have already converted into stock you are not selling. Decide, item by item: use it, discount it, or stop buying it.

04Your menu contains dishes nobody has ever questioned

Every menu carries passengers. Dishes that have been there since opening, that nobody has re-costed since the supplier changed, that stay on because removing them feels like a decision and leaving them does not.

Menu engineering sorts every dish on two axes: how often it sells, and how much margin it contributes. That gives four groups, and two of them need action.

The obvious problem is the dish that is both unpopular and unprofitable — easy to remove once you see it in a grid, and usually a relief for the kitchen. The expensive problem is the opposite: the dish that sells beautifully and earns almost nothing. It feels like a success. It is on every table. And every plate that leaves the pass is subsidised by something else on the menu.

The check

Plot every dish: units sold on one axis, contribution margin in rupiah per dish on the other. Not margin percentage — rupiah. A 70% margin on a dish that sells four times a week is worth less than a 55% margin on one that sells forty.

05Nobody owns the number

This is the one that makes the other four permanent.

In most operations, cost control sits in the gap between two departments. The chef assumes finance is tracking it, because finance produces the reports. Finance assumes the chef is managing it, because the chef controls the spending. Both are reasonable assumptions, and the result is that no single person wakes up on Monday responsible for a specific figure.

What closes this gap is not software and not a longer report. It is profit traceability: the principle that every significant movement in cost should be attributable to a decision somebody made, and that somebody should be named.

The check

Pick one number — theoretical versus actual food cost is the right place to start. Give it one named owner. Review it weekly, not monthly, in a meeting that lasts fifteen minutes. Monthly review means a problem runs for four weeks before anyone sees it.

These are not theoretical. Rebuilding recipe costing and purchasing controls across four outlets of a café group in Canggu moved food cost variance from 39% to 29% in 90 days. A separate engagement delivered a 5.1% COGS reduction in six weeks — with no price increases, purely through systematised inventory and recipe discipline. Figures are real; venue identities are protected under NDA.

None of this requires new software

It is worth saying plainly, because the usual response to a cost problem is to buy a system. Every check above can be done with a spreadsheet and a disciplined week. What they have in common is not technology — it is that each one converts something invisible into something you can look at.

That is the whole job. Operational chaos is not a lack of effort; it is a lack of visibility. Once a leak has a name, a number and an owner, it stops being a mystery and becomes a decision.
